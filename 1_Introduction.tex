\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Randomized algorithms are routinely used in simulation, machine learning, network protocols, and exact sampling. Their correctness has two independent dimensions. \emph{Partial correctness} states that, whenever an algorithm returns, its result has the intended distribution. \emph{Almost-sure termination} (AST) states that it returns with probability one. Only their combination establishes total correctness: without AST, a sampler may produce the right distribution on terminating runs while losing probability mass to divergence.

AST proofs are subtle. A probabilistic loop may terminate almost surely even though its expected runtime is infinite and even though no globally bounded ranking function exists. The symmetric random walk on the non-negative integers is the canonical example: from $x>0$, move to $x-1$ or $x+1$ with equal probability and stop at zero. It reaches zero with probability one, yet its expected hitting time is infinite. Classical finite-state or bounded-variant arguments therefore do not suffice.

Majumdar and Sathiyanarayana recently introduced a sound and relatively complete rule for probabilistic termination at the level of probabilistic control-flow graphs (pCFGs)~\cite{DBLP:journals/pacmpl/MajumdarS25}. Their rule combines a supermartingale with a natural-valued variant. The supermartingale controls the probability of escaping to regions in which the variant becomes large; within each of its sublevel sets, the variant is bounded and decreases with a uniformly positive probability. This is a strong semantic characterization of AST.

Applying this pCFG rule to a source program introduces additional proof structure. A loop iteration that is conceptually a single sampling step becomes a collection of locations and transitions. The two certificate functions take location--valuation pairs as arguments, often requiring different symbolic cases for assignments, guards, and probabilistic branches. For the Fast Dice Roller (FDR), for example, a simple source-level observation about the growth of $v-c$ turns into a location-indexed variant with several cases. This overhead concerns the representation of the certificates, rather than a need to enumerate individual valuations. It is especially unfortunate for exact samplers, whose functional correctness is already naturally expressed by \emph{distributional invariants} and distribution-transformer semantics~\cite{zilken2026verifyingsamplingalgorithmsdistributional}.

\paragraph{Our approach.}
We lift the two-certificate pCFG rule to the source-program level, summarizing the complete effect of one loop-body execution by its post-distribution. Let $C=\while{b}{B}$, where $B$ is loop-free. The rule requires:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item a distributional invariant $I$ closed under one loop iteration;
  \item a non-negative supermartingale $v$ whose expected value does not increase after executing $B$;
  \item a natural-valued variant $u$ that, with probability at least $\varepsilon>0$, is smaller after executing $B$; and
  \item boundedness of $u$ in every sublevel set of $v$.
\end{enumerate}
All obligations are stated over source states and the distribution transformer $\post{B}$. The rule therefore reasons at the semantic granularity at which programmers understand a probabilistic loop: one iteration at a time.

Source-level AST rules already exist, notably the rule of McIver et al.~\cite{10.1145/3158121}. Our contribution is a program-level formulation of the separate supermartingale and variant certificates of Majumdar and Sathiyanarayana, together with its metatheory and integration with distributional invariants. The separation itself comes from the pCFG rule; the new formulation makes that approach available through obligations over a complete source-level iteration.

\paragraph{Why sampling algorithms?}
Exact sampling algorithms provide a natural setting in which to evaluate AST proof rules. Their loop invariants often describe entire distributions rather than merely state predicates; rejection sampling introduces restarts that defeat na\"ive pointwise descent arguments; and total correctness requires the AST proof to compose with a pre-existing output-distribution proof. Our formulation directly supports this workflow. Reachability information is extracted from the support of a distributional invariant, while the richer equal-mass constraints used for partial correctness can be reused unchanged.

After a one-dimensional random walk introduces the rule's mechanism, we study three samplers with complementary roles. First, the Fast Dice Roller (FDR)~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1304-1916} samples uniformly from $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ using fair bits. We give both pCFG-level and program-level proofs, making the additional location-dependent cases and offsets explicit. This comparison demonstrates a reduction in the bookkeeping needed to express the same termination argument. Second, the Fast Loaded Dice Roller (FLDR)~\cite{DBLP:conf/aistats/SaadFRM20} samples from a finite distribution with rational probabilities using a compact decision tree and rejection. Its program-level proof handles progress and reset in one body step. Third, the Han--Hoshi sampler repeatedly refines a dyadic interval until it lies entirely within a target-distribution interval. Its certificate counts the target boundaries inside the current interval. FLDR and Han--Hoshi demonstrate applicability through program-level proofs; the explicit comparison between the two proof rules is provided by FDR. The resulting evidence concerns certificate structure and manual proof obligations, without a measured claim about proof-development or solver runtime.

For FDR and FLDR, prior work already supplies distributional invariants and AST arguments~\cite{zilken2026verifyingsamplingalgorithmsdistributional}. We reuse these verification problems to show how a general program-level AST rule fits the existing distributional proof framework.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item We present a new program-level proof rule for AST of probabilistic while-loops with loop-free bodies. The rule is phrased entirely in distribution-transformer semantics and cleanly separates the supermartingale and variant obligations (\Cref{sec:rule}).
 \item We establish soundness by compiling program-level certificates into certificates for the relatively complete pCFG rule. Conversely, we derive program-level certificates from pCFG certificates, yielding relative completeness with respect to the underlying first-order arithmetic theory (\Cref{sec:metatheory}).
 \item We illustrate the mechanism on the one-dimensional random walk and explain how the source-level rule removes location-dependent bookkeeping (\Cref{sec:walk}).
 \item We give parallel pCFG- and program-level AST proofs for FDR. The comparison exhibits the location-dependent certificate cases and offsets eliminated at program level, and shows how to reuse an existing distributional invariant (\Cref{sec:fdr}).
 \item We prove AST of FLDR and Han--Hoshi at program level. FLDR captures rejection and restart through one body-step argument over its decision tree, while Han--Hoshi uses a certificate based on the target-distribution boundaries contained in the current dyadic interval. These case studies demonstrate the rule's applicability to distinct sampling mechanisms. For FDR and FLDR, the AST proofs combine with existing distributional invariants to recover total correctness (\Cref{sec:fldr,sec:HanHoshi}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.}
The present rule targets a single while-loop with a loop-free body and no nondeterministic choice. This fragment already captures the characteristic structure of many sampling algorithms, where all potentially unbounded computation is concentrated in one main loop, while preprocessing and the individual loop iterations terminate. Restricting the body to be loop-free therefore still covers a broad and practically relevant class of samplers, while making each iteration a finite distribution-transformer step and keeping the correspondence to pCFG paths transparent.

The restriction also provides a natural basis for treating nested loops compositionally. In particular, an inner loop can first be verified for termination and its semantics summarized; the resulting terminating command can then be treated as part of the body of the surrounding loop. Developing such a compositional treatment systematically, as well as extending the rule to nondeterministic choice and certificate synthesis, is left to future work (\Cref{sec:conclusion}).
